Brand & interface guidelines

Making marketing easier.

The visual language behind jamieclarke.online: a navy-and-orange system built on hard-edged borders, offset shadows and plain-English copy. Nothing here is decoration for its own sake — every rule exists so a service business can read the page, trust the person, and take one clear next step.

Navy #1B3C7F Orange #E2613D Cream #FBF5EA Gabarito + Maven Pro 2px borders, hard shadows

Five principles

If a decision isn't covered by a rule further down this page, decide it with these.

1

Confident, not corporate

Solid borders and hard shadows over soft gradients and glassy blur. The interface should feel drawn with a marker pen, not rendered in a boardroom.

2

One next step per screen

Orange is reserved for the single action that matters most in view. If two orange buttons compete, one of them is wrong.

3

Plain English, always

Copy is design material. Short sentences, no jargon, no waffle. Say what the thing does, then say what happens when you click it.

4

Warmth over polish

Cream backgrounds, hand-drawn stick figures and a slight tilt on a card do more for trust than another stock photo of a handshake.

5

Order is the message

The Perfect Customer Journey runs six stages in sequence. Where content is genuinely sequential, number it. Where it isn't, don't fake it.

Colour

Three colours carry the brand and three support it. Navy is the ink, orange is the action, cream is the resting surface. Teal and yellow appear in small doses only — a stage marker, a sticker, a highlight — never as a page background.

Rules

  • Headings are set in sentence case with a full stop when they read as a statement (“Sound familiar?”, “Things I'm working on.”). Title Case is reserved for proper nouns like The Marketing Memo.
  • Body copy stays between 60 and 70 characters per line. Long paragraphs get broken up, not justified.
  • Bold inside a paragraph is navy, never orange — it marks the sentence worth remembering.
  • Never set body copy in Gabarito, and never set a button in Maven Pro.
  • Weights in use: Gabarito 700 / 800 / 900, Maven Pro 400 / 500 / 600 / 700. Nothing else.

Rules

  • One orange button per viewport. A second action is always the white secondary.
  • Minimum tap target 44px tall — 14px vertical padding at 16px text clears it.
  • Arrows point right and sit after the label with an 8px gap. No arrow on form submits.
  • Full-width buttons are for forms and mobile menus only.
  • Flat buttons (no border, soft shadow) exist for dense UI inside a card — cookie bars, inline signup rows — where a chunky shadow would be too loud.

Cards

White fill, navy border, chunky shadow, 16px radius. A card is a link target: the whole surface is clickable, and the hover lift is the affordance.

Illustration

One recurring character does all the explaining. He is drawn in black ink on white, wears a baseball cap in every frame, and never changes proportions between drawings — that consistency is what makes the illustrations read as a set rather than a pile of clip art.

Anatomy

Reference construction. Head diameter is the unit: the figure stands 3.5–4 heads tall, and every prop in a scene is sized against him at realistic human scale. If he's next to a lighthouse, the lighthouse towers over him.

Examples

Rules

✓ Always

  • Baseball cap on, every single time. White with cross-hatched shading, never filled with colour.
  • Round head, slightly oversized, white inside with a black outline. Dot eyes, curved mouth, short angled brows.
  • Clean white background, black ink pen, a hand-drawn wobble rather than perfect geometry.
  • Realistic scale between the character and everything around him.
  • Any other figures in the scene are smaller and simpler than he is.

× Never

  • No colour on the character — not his cap, not his clothes, not another stick figure.
  • No third accent colour. Orange and navy are the only two permitted, on props and environment only.
  • No nose, no ears, no hair.
  • No 3D, no photorealism, no clip art. Cartoon line illustration only.
  • No long text in the artwork. Labels are short and bold or they don't go in.

Colour is used sparingly: a mostly black-and-white frame with a few accented objects is the target. If the whole scene is orange, the accent has stopped meaning anything.

Generation prompt

New illustrations are generated from the reference set with the prompt below. Attach the existing images every time — they are the definitive guide, and the prompt only holds if the model can see them.

VISUAL STYLE INSTRUCTIONS

You generate stick figure illustrations based on prompts I give you. Your only job is
to create the image while keeping the character and style 100% consistent with the
attached reference images.

THE CHARACTER
Study the attached reference images. This is the definitive guide. Match it exactly
every time. Key details that must never change:
  - Round head, slightly oversized relative to body. White/empty inside, black outline.
  - Baseball cap ALWAYS present. White with black cross-hatching/sketch shading lines.
    Never coloured.
  - Simple face: small dot eyes, curved line mouth, short angled eyebrows. No nose,
    no ears.
  - Roughly 3.5-4 head-heights tall.
  - NEVER add any colour to the character. He is black ink on white only.

PROPORTIONS
The character must always be at realistic human scale relative to the objects and
environment around him. If he's next to a lighthouse, the lighthouse towers over him.
If he's at a desk, he's normal desk height. Don't make him oversized relative to the
scene.

COLOURS
Only two accent colours are ever permitted (plus black and white):
  #E4623E (warm orange-red)
  #1A4081 (deep navy blue)
These go on objects, props, and environmental elements ONLY. Never on the character
or other stick figures. Use them sparingly - a few coloured accents on a mostly black
and white image is the goal.

STYLE
  - Clean white background
  - Black ink pen, hand-drawn feel with slight imperfection
  - Cartoon/illustration style, not clipart, not 3D, not photorealistic
  - Other stick figures in the scene should be simpler and smaller than the main
    character
  - Keep any text labels short and bold

THE PERFECT CUSTOMER JOURNEY
If the PCJ, Perfect Customer Journey or stages are mentioned, they are the 6 stages:
  1. Get Noticed   2. Connect   3. Engage
  4. Convert       5. Deliver & Wow   6. Create Fans
One thing to settle. The illustration prompt uses #E4623E / #1A4081, while the website ships #E2613D / #1B3C7F. They are a shade apart — close enough to look like a mistake side by side, far enough that an illustration dropped next to a button won't quite match. Pick one pair and use it in both places; the site's values are the safer default, since they're already compiled into the build.

Other imagery

Real, unposed, outdoors or at the desk. Portraits crop to 4:5 on mobile and 3:4 on desktop with a 16px radius. No stock photography of anyone else, ever.

Product screenshots

Project cards use a 2:1 well on cream. Screenshots sit square to the frame, no perspective mockups, no floating device shells.

Decorative shapes

The soft orange blur behind a hero, the rotated cream slab behind a portrait: allowed at 5–10% opacity, never behind body text.

Voice & tone

British English, second person, contractions throughout. Jamie talks to one business owner at a time — not to a market.

✓ Do

  • Name the reader's situation before offering the fix: “You're posting on social media but the phone isn't ringing.”
  • Use one concrete metaphor and follow it through — the jigsaw, the half-built bridge.
  • Keep sentences short. Fragments are fine. They land.
  • Be specific about what's free and what isn't. Say the price of the next step.
  • Let a little personality in — the golf, the Manchester United line — then get back to the point.

× Don't

  • Don't use agency language: leverage, synergy, solutions, bespoke, cutting-edge.
  • Don't blame the reader. The problem is the order things were done in, not their ability.
  • Don't stack three adjectives where one verb will do.
  • Don't promise numbers you can't evidence. Testimonials carry the proof instead.
  • Don't write a heading that could sit on anyone else's website.
You're not bad at marketing. You're just doing things in the wrong order.

The house line. It sets the tone for everything else: sympathetic about the problem, blunt about the cause, confident about the fix.

Accessibility

The chunky style is high-contrast by nature. These are the rules that keep it that way.

  • Focus is always visible. A 3px orange outline at 3px offset, on every interactive element. Never remove it without replacing it.
  • Orange text is bold or large. At 3.49:1 it fails AA for small body text — see the contrast table.
  • Colour is never the only signal. Links inside prose are bold and underline on hover; step markers carry a number as well as a fill.
  • Motion is optional. All hover lifts and the logo marquee respect prefers-reduced-motion.
  • Headings nest properly. One h1 per page, no level skipped for visual reasons — size comes from a class, not from the tag.
  • Tap targets are 44px. Including nav links and the footer's social icons.
  • Every image has alt text that describes the idea, not the file: “Stick figure looking at three bridges — only one fully built across the gap.”
